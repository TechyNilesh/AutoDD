\begin{abstract}

Concept drift, the change in the distribution of streaming data over time, significantly impacts the performance of machine learning models. While numerous drift detection methods have been proposed, selecting and configuring the most suitable detector for a specific data stream remains a challenging task. Existing AutoML frameworks for streaming data that automate model selection rely on fixed drift detection strategies, leaving a critical gap in automation. This paper introduces \textit{AutoDD} (\underline{A}utomated \underline{D}rift \underline{D}etector), a novel framework that jointly automates detector selection and hyperparameter configuration using a hierarchical learning approach. AutoDD extracts comprehensive online meta-features and employs a two-level hierarchical selection algorithm to adaptively choose both the detector and its configuration. Theoretical analysis provides sublinear regret bounds ensuring convergence to optimal performance. Comprehensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that AutoDD consistently outperforms state-of-the-art detectors while maintaining computational efficiency.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Concept drift, where the statistical properties of streaming data evolve over time, poses a significant challenge in data stream mining \cite{Gama2014Survey,Bifet2010MOA}, incremental learning \cite{Losing2018IncrementalOnline}, and non-stationary learning \cite{Lu2019Learning}. As data distributions change, machine learning models experience degraded performance, necessitating timely detection and adaptation. This phenomenon manifests in various forms, including sudden (abrupt) drift, gradual drift, incremental drift, and recurring drift, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:type_of_drift}. For instance, an electricity price prediction model may encounter sudden drift when market regulations change abruptly, experience gradual drift as consumer behaviour shifts over seasons, or observe recurring drift driven by daily demand cycles~\cite{Lu2019Learning}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\columnwidth]{images/concept/type_of_drift_detection.png}
    \caption{Types of concept drifts.}
    \label{fig:type_of_drift}
\end{figure}

Over the past two decades, numerous drift detection methods have been developed to address this challenge, including error rate-based approaches such as DDM \cite{Gama2004DDM}, ADWIN \cite{Bifet2007ADWIN}, and ADDM \cite{Mayaki2022ADDM}, distribution-based methods like KSWIN \cite{Raab2020KSWIN}, uncertainty-based methods such as PUDD \cite{Lu2025PUDD}, and meta-learning techniques like Meta-ADD \cite{Yu2021MetaADD}. However, a fundamental challenge remains unresolved in systematically selecting and configuring the most appropriate drift detector for a given data stream.

Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) has revolutionised the field by addressing similar selection challenges in batch learning scenarios \cite{Hutter2019AutoML}. Recent advancements have extended AutoML to streaming data, enabling automatic selection and configuration of classifiers \cite{Celik2023OAML,Verma2024ASML}. However, these systems treat drift detection as a fixed component, requiring manual specification and leaving a critical gap in the automation framework.

To bridge this gap, we introduce \textbf{AutoDD} (\textbf{A}utomated \textbf{D}rift \textbf{D}etector). This modular framework automates drift detector selection and configuration using a hierarchical learning approach, compatible with any model or AutoML pipeline.

\begin{itemize}
\item We propose a two-level hierarchical selection algorithm using LinUCB \cite{Li2010LinUCB} and Thompson Sampling \cite{Agrawal2012Thompson} that adaptively selects detectors and their hyperparameters based on online meta-features.
\item We formalise detector selection with a theoretical analysis that provides sublinear regret bounds for the proxy selection objective optimised by the selection layer.
\item Extensive evaluation demonstrates AutoDD outperforms state-of-the-art detectors while maintaining computational efficiency.
\end{itemize}

The rest of this paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} presents the related work. Section~\ref{sec:method} provides the details of our proposed AutoDD framework. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} describes the experimental setup. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the experimental results and analysis. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses key design decisions and limitations. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Drift detection methods can be broadly categorised into three paradigms. \textbf{Error rate-based methods} monitor fluctuations in classifier error rates, triggering alarms when changes are statistically significant. DDM \cite{Gama2004DDM} pioneered the warning-drift framework by monitoring prediction accuracy under binomial distribution assumptions, followed by EDDM \cite{BaenaGarcia2006EDDM} which focuses on distances between errors to detect gradual drift, HDDM \cite{FriasBlanco2015Hoeffding} which applies Hoeffding's inequality with exponentially weighted moving averages, and ADWIN \cite{Bifet2007ADWIN} which maintains adaptive sliding windows that automatically shrink when distribution changes are detected. Page-Hinkley \cite{Page1954Continuous} applies cumulative sum control charts to detect mean shifts in sequential data. Recent advances include MDDM \cite{Pesaranghader2017MDDM}, which uses McDiarmid's inequality for shorter detection delays with tighter concentration bounds, and ADDM \cite{Mayaki2022ADDM}, which models error rates as SETAR time series \cite{Tsay1989TestingAM} to achieve lower false alarm rates. \textbf{Distribution-based methods} compare data distributions across windows by quantifying dissimilarity between new and historical data distributions \cite{Kifer2004ChangeDetection}, including approaches based on information theory \cite{Dasu2006InformationTheoretic}, competence models \cite{Lu2014CompetenceModels}, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (KSWIN) \cite{Raab2020KSWIN} which detects changes in univariate feature distributions. Fully unsupervised, model-independent variants such as the image-based detector IBDD \cite{Souza2021IBDD} render high-dimensional windows as greyscale images and monitor their mean squared deviation, enabling label-free detection on fast, high-dimensional streams. \textbf{Uncertainty and learning-based methods} address limitations of error-rate monitoring: PUDD \cite{Lu2025PUDD} leverages prediction confidence for earlier detection by monitoring changes in prediction uncertainty distributions, while Meta-ADD \cite{Yu2021MetaADD} employs prototypical networks to classify drift types via meta-learning on diverse drift scenarios.

Recent developments in automated algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimisation (CASH) \cite{Hutter2019AutoML} have been extended to streaming scenarios~\cite{kulbach2022evolution}. However, all existing streaming AutoML systems assume a pre-selected drift detector, requiring manual specification and leaving a critical gap in the automation framework. Despite advances in both drift detection and streaming AutoML, drift detector selection remains manual. Our work fills this gap by extending AutoML to automatic drift detector selection and configuration.

\section{Automated Drift Detector}
\label{sec:method}

AutoDD employs an adaptive, closed-loop architecture to dynamically select and configure drift detectors. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}, this architecture operates through several key steps. First, the system initialises the detector portfolio with standard hyperparameters and sets up the learning parameters. During online operation, meta-features characterising stream velocity, stationarity, and temporal patterns are continuously extracted from the incoming data stream. These meta-features serve as context for the LinUCB agent, which selects a detector from the portfolio. Thompson Sampling then chooses a hyperparameter configuration for the selected detector. The configured detector monitors the stream and triggers model adaptation upon detecting drift. Finally, performance feedback is computed based on detection outcomes, which updates both the LinUCB and Thompson Sampling components to refine future selections. This adaptive learning cycle enables AutoDD to improve its detector selections over time.

\subsection{Hierarchical Learning Approach}

AutoDD uses a two-level hierarchical selection strategy that jointly adapts detector choice and hyperparameter configuration while maintaining computational efficiency. This decomposition avoids directly treating all $K \times C$ detector-configuration pairs as a single flat action space.

\textbf{Level 1 - Detector Selection via LinUCB:} We formulate detector selection as a contextual multi-armed bandit problem where context is $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t$ (meta-features), arms are $K$ detectors, and reward is detection performance. LinUCB \cite{Li2010LinUCB} assumes linear reward:
\begin{equation}
r_{i,t} = \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i + \epsilon_t
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the unknown parameter vector for detector $i$, with $m$ being the meta-feature dimensionality. The algorithm selects the detector with the highest upper confidence bound:
\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.9\columnwidth}{!}{$
i_t = \arg\max_{i \in [K]} \left( \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i + \alpha_{\text{ucb}} \sqrt{\boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \mathbf{A}_i^{-1} \boldsymbol{\phi}_t} \right)
$}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i = \mathbf{A}_i^{-1} \mathbf{b}_i$ is the ridge regression estimate. Here, $\mathbf{A}_i = \lambda_{\text{reg}}\mathbf{I} + \sum_{s:i_s=i} \boldsymbol{\phi}_s \boldsymbol{\phi}_s^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is the regularised design matrix with regularisation parameter $\lambda_{\text{reg}} > 0$, and $\mathbf{b}_i = \sum_{s:i_s=i} r_s \boldsymbol{\phi}_s \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the cumulative reward-weighted context vector for detector $i$. The parameter $\alpha_{\text{ucb}} > 0$ controls exploration-exploitation balance.

\textbf{Level 2 - Hyperparameter Selection via Thompson Sampling:} Given the selected detector $D_{i_t}$, we adaptively configure its hyperparameters using Thompson Sampling \cite{Agrawal2012Thompson}. For each configuration $c \in [C] = \{1, \ldots, C\}$ of detector $i$, we maintain Beta distribution parameters $(a_{i,c}, b_{i,c})$ initialised to $(1, 1)$. At each window, we sample:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ts_sample}
\psi_{i_t,c} \sim \text{Beta}(a_{i_t,c}, b_{i_t,c}) \quad \forall c \in [C]
\end{equation}
and select configuration:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ts_select}
c_t = \arg\max_c \psi_{i_t,c}
\end{equation}

After observing reward $r_t$, we update:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:ts_update}
a_{i_t,c_t} &\gets a_{i_t,c_t} + \tilde{r}_t \\
b_{i_t,c_t} &\gets b_{i_t,c_t} + (1 - \tilde{r}_t)
\end{align}
where $\tilde{r}_t = (r_t + 1)/2$ normalises the reward to $[0,1]$. %This cascading approach provides tractability (only $K$ arms at Level 1), specialization (each detector's hyperparameters tuned independently), and fast adaptation (Thompson Sampling quickly identifies effective configurations).

\subsection{Evaluation and Adaptation}

Evaluating detector performance without ground-truth drift labels is challenging. Following standard practice, we use model accuracy drop as a proxy metric. The reward at time $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t = \begin{cases}
0.5 + \min(\Delta a_t \cdot 2, 0.5) & d_t=1, \Delta a_t > \tau_{\text{sig}} \\
-0.3 & d_t=1, \Delta a_t \leq \tau_{\text{sig}} \\
-0.5 - \min(\Delta a_t, 0.5) & d_t=0, \Delta a_t > \tau_{\text{sig}} \\
0.1 & d_t=0, \Delta a_t \leq \tau_{\text{sig}}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $d_t \in \{0,1\}$ indicates drift detection at time $t$, $\Delta a_t = a_{t-w} - a_t$ is the accuracy drop between consecutive windows, and $\tau_{\text{sig}} = 0.05$ is the significance threshold. The reward function penalizes both false positives and false negatives while rewarding correct drift detections and stable periods. We note that an accuracy-based proxy couples detector quality with the downstream learner, label noise, and the evaluation window. Two design choices limit the resulting variance: the drop $\Delta a_t$ is measured over a window of $w$ instances rather than per-instance, smoothing transient noise, and the threshold $\tau_{\text{sig}}$ suppresses rewards from sub-significant fluctuations so that noise-driven dips do not reinforce spurious detections. Because the reward enters the bandit update only as a bounded scalar in $[-1,1]$, occasional misattributed rewards perturb the posteriors gradually rather than abruptly. The regret analysis in Section~\ref{sec:method} is therefore stated for this bounded proxy reward; interpreting proxy regret as true detector quality requires the proxy-fidelity assumption introduced below.

Upon drift detection, the model undergoes a complete reset. Let $\mathcal{M}_t$ denote the model state at time $t$. When drift is detected ($d_t = 1$), we perform:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}_t \gets \mathcal{M}_0
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}_0$ represents the initial untrained state. The model then resumes incremental learning from subsequent instances:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}_{t+s} \gets \text{Update}(\mathcal{M}_{t+s-1}, (\mathbf{x}_{t+s}, y_{t+s})) \quad \forall s > 0
\end{equation}

The complete procedure is formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:autodd}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{AutoDD}
\label{alg:autodd}
\KwIn{Stream $\mathcal{S}$, portfolio $\mathcal{D}$, window $w$; exploration $\alpha_{\text{ucb}}$, regularisation $\lambda_{\text{reg}}$, configs per detector $C$}
\KwOut{Drift signals $\{d_t\}_{t=1}^{\infty}$}
Initialise $\mathbf{A}_i \gets \lambda_{\text{reg}}\mathbf{I}_m$, $\mathbf{b}_i \gets \mathbf{0}$ for all $i \in [K]$\;
Initialise $a_{i,c} \gets 1$, $b_{i,c} \gets 1$ for all $i \in [K], c \in [C]$\;
Initialise model $\mathcal{M}_0$, sliding window $W \gets \emptyset$\;
\ForEach{instance $(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t) \in \mathcal{S}$}{
    $W \gets W \cup \{(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t)\}$\;
    \If{$|W| = w$}{
        $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t \gets \textsc{Extract}(W)$ \tcp*{meta-features}
        \For{$i = 1$ \KwTo $K$}{
            $\text{UCB}_i \gets \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i + \alpha_{\text{ucb}} \sqrt{\boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \mathbf{A}_i^{-1} \boldsymbol{\phi}_t}$ \tcp*{$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i = \mathbf{A}_i^{-1}\mathbf{b}_i$}
        }
        $i_t \gets \arg\max_i \text{UCB}_i$ \tcp*{select detector}
        \For{$c = 1$ \KwTo $C$}{
            Sample $\psi_c \sim \text{Beta}(a_{i_t,c}, b_{i_t,c})$\;
        }
        $c_t \gets \arg\max_c \psi_c$ \tcp*{select configuration}
        $d_t \gets D_{i_t}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i_t,c_t}).\textsc{Detect}(W)$\;
        \If{$d_t = 1$}{
            $\mathcal{M}_t \gets \mathcal{M}_0$ \tcp*{reset model}
        }
        Compute reward $r_t$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}\;
        $\mathbf{A}_{i_t} \gets \mathbf{A}_{i_t} + \boldsymbol{\phi}_t \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top$;\quad $\mathbf{b}_{i_t} \gets \mathbf{b}_{i_t} + r_t \boldsymbol{\phi}_t$\;
        $\tilde{r}_t \gets (r_t + 1)/2$\;
        $a_{i_t,c_t} \gets a_{i_t,c_t} + \tilde{r}_t$;\quad $b_{i_t,c_t} \gets b_{i_t,c_t} + (1-\tilde{r}_t)$\;
        $W \gets \emptyset$\;
    }
}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Computational Complexity}
AutoDD has per-window complexity of $O(wd + Km^2)$ for the incrementally maintained meta-features used in our implementation, where $w$ is the AutoDD window size, $d$ is the input feature dimensionality, $K$ is the number of detectors, and $m=15$ is the meta-feature count. The main costs are meta-feature extraction ($O(wd)$) and LinUCB matrix operations ($O(Km^2)$). Space complexity is $O(Km^2 + wd)$ for storing $K$ covariance matrices of size $m \times m$ and the sliding window buffer.

\subsection{Theoretical Analysis}

We formalize the optimisation objective as a streaming detector selection problem and provide proxy-regret bounds for the hierarchical learning approach. Our analysis focuses on the selection mechanism, showing that the average proxy suboptimality vanishes under the stated contextual-bandit assumptions.

\textbf{Problem Formulation.} A data stream $\mathcal{S} = \{(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^{\infty}$ exhibits concept drift when distributions change over time:
\begin{equation}
\exists t, t' : P_t(\mathbf{x}, y) \neq P_{t'}(\mathbf{x}, y)
\end{equation}

Given a detector portfolio $\mathcal{D} = \{D_1, \ldots, D_K\}$ where each $D_i$ has configuration space $\mathcal{H}_i$, AutoDD addresses the streaming detector selection and configuration problem. Inspired by the CASH formulation \cite{Hutter2019AutoML}, we jointly optimise the detector choice and its hyperparameters:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cash}
\resizebox{0.95\columnwidth}{!}{$
(D^*_t, \boldsymbol{\eta}^*_t) = \arg\max_{D_i \in \mathcal{D}, \boldsymbol{\eta} \in \mathcal{H}_i} \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}(D_i(\boldsymbol{\eta}), \mathcal{S}_t) \mid \boldsymbol{\phi}_t]
$}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t$ is the stream context, $\mathcal{S}_t$ denotes the stream segment at time $t$, $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ is the expectation operator, and $\mathcal{M}$ denotes the downstream model performance.

\textbf{Assumptions.} We adopt three standard assumptions from the contextual bandit literature \cite{Abbasi2011Improved,Lattimore2020Bandit}, together with a fidelity condition (A4) that links our accuracy-based proxy reward to true detection quality:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{(A1) Bounded features:} $\|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_2 \leq L$ for all $t$, satisfied by $[0,1]$ normalisation with $L=\sqrt{m}$.
\item \textbf{(A2) Linear reward:} For each detector $i$, there exists $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^* \in \mathbb{R}^m$ with $\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*\|_2 \leq S$ such that $\mathbb{E}[r_{i,t} \mid \boldsymbol{\phi}_t] = \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*$, where $S > 0$ is a constant upper bound on the parameter norms.
\item \textbf{(A3) Sub-Gaussian noise:} Reward noise is $\sigma_{\text{noise}}$-sub-Gaussian, satisfied with $\sigma_{\text{noise}} = 1$ for bounded rewards in $[-1,1]$.
\item \textbf{(A4) Proxy fidelity:} The windowed accuracy-drop reward (Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}) is, in expectation, monotone in true detection quality, so that a high proxy-reward detector-configuration pair is also high quality under the objective in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cash}. The bounds below are stated with respect to this proxy objective; reading proxy regret as evidence of convergence towards a high-quality detector additionally relies on A4 as a modelling assumption.
\end{itemize}

These assumptions characterise the \emph{selection layer} under the standard linear contextual-bandit abstraction; they are not claims about the full loop spanning detection, adaptation, and learning. A2 in particular treats the windowed reward as linear in the meta-features in expectation, an approximation we do not prove directly but examine empirically through the selection-accuracy trend in Table~\ref{tab:conv_main}.

\emph{Remark (stationarity under drift).} Although the data distribution $P_t$ is non-stationary by construction, A2 requires only that the \emph{conditional} reward map $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t \mapsto \mathbb{E}[r_{i,t}\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}_t]$ be stationary, not the marginal $P_t$. This is the intended role of the meta-features: $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t$ is designed to encode the current stream regime (velocity, stationarity, and temporal structure), so changes in $P_t$ can be represented through the context rather than through time-varying $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*$. A detector well matched to a recurring regime should earn comparable reward whenever that regime recurs, keeping $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*$ approximately fixed across time. When this abstraction is imperfect, i.e., the reward map itself drifts, sliding-window or discounted LinUCB variants could instead be analysed using dynamic-regret tools with a variation budget $B_T = \sum_{t} \|\boldsymbol{\theta}^*_{i,t} - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*_{i,t-1}\|_2$ \cite{Lattimore2020Bandit}. The monotonic rise of proxy-oracle selection accuracy in Table~\ref{tab:conv_main} is consistent with the stationary approximation on the streams we study.

The following result gives the detector-selection proxy regret for LinUCB under the stationary linear abstraction.

\textbf{Theorem 1 (Level 1 Regret).} Under assumptions A1--A3, with exploration parameter $\alpha_{\text{ucb}} = \sigma_{\text{noise}}\sqrt{m \log(KT/\delta_{\text{conf}})} + S$ where $T$ is the time horizon and $\delta_{\text{conf}} \in (0,1)$ is the confidence parameter, LinUCB detector selection achieves with probability at least $1 - \delta_{\text{conf}}$:
\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.95\columnwidth}{!}{$
R_1(T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \max_{i} \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^* - \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_{i_t}^* \right) = O(\sqrt{KmT \log T})
$}
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} The proof follows the disjoint-arm LinUCB analysis of \cite{Abbasi2011Improved}. By the self-normalised martingale inequality (Theorem 1, \cite{Abbasi2011Improved}), the true parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*$ lies within the confidence ellipsoid $\mathcal{C}_{i,t} = \{\boldsymbol{\theta}: \|\boldsymbol{\theta} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i\|_{\mathbf{A}_i} \leq \alpha_{\text{ucb}}\}$ with probability $1-\delta_{\text{conf}}$. Under this event, UCB selection ensures $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_{i_t}^* \geq \max_i \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^* - 2\alpha_{\text{ucb}}\|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i_t}^{-1}}$. Applying the elliptical potential lemma \cite{Dani2008Stochastic} separately to each detector estimator and then summing over detectors gives the additional $\sqrt{K}$ factor, yielding $R_1(T)=O(\sqrt{KmT\log T})$ under the standard convention that treats the confidence radius $\alpha_{\text{ucb}}$ as constant. The full three-step derivation, including the dimension-explicit form $\tilde{O}(m\sqrt{KT})$ obtained by retaining $\alpha_{\text{ucb}}$, is given in Appendix~\ref{appendix:proofs}. \hfill $\square$

Thompson Sampling subsequently balances exploration and exploitation for hyperparameter configuration.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (Level 2 Proxy Regret).} For Thompson Sampling on $C$ configurations per detector under the standard Bernoulli-sampling reduction for bounded rewards, with $N_i(T)$ denoting the number of times detector $i \in [K]$ is selected over $T$ rounds, the expected cumulative proxy regret satisfies:
\begin{equation}
R_2(T) = \sum_{i=1}^{K} O(\sqrt{C \cdot N_i(T) \log N_i(T)})
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} For each detector $i \in [K]$, conditioned on detector $i$ being selected, hyperparameter selection reduces to a $C$-armed bandit with bounded rewards. We normalise $r_t \in [-1,1]$ to $\tilde{r}_t = (r_t+1)/2 \in [0,1]$. The standard reduction draws a Bernoulli outcome with mean $\tilde{r}_t$ and updates the Beta posterior with that sampled outcome; by Theorem 1 of \cite{Agrawal2013Further}, Thompson Sampling achieves expected regret $O(\sqrt{CN_i(T)\log N_i(T)})$ over $N_i(T)$ rounds. Since $\sum_i N_i(T) = T$, summing across all detectors yields the stated bound. The fractional update in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ts_update} is the conditional expectation of this reduction and is used as a stable implementation heuristic rather than a separate regret theorem. The full reduction-and-aggregation argument is given in Appendix~\ref{appendix:proofs}. \hfill $\square$

Under the same proxy-reward abstraction, the combined regret remains sublinear, implying that average per-round proxy suboptimality vanishes over time.

\textbf{Corollary (Combined Proxy Regret).} Let $(i^*_t, c^*_t)$ denote the per-context proxy-oracle detector-configuration pair at round $t$. If the hierarchical proxy objective decomposes into detector-selection and within-detector configuration terms, then the total regret can be written as $R(T) = R_1(T) + R_2(T)$, where $R_1(T)$ captures detector-selection suboptimality and $R_2(T)$ captures configuration-selection suboptimality. The combined bound is:
\begin{equation}
R(T) = O(\sqrt{KmT \log T} + \sqrt{CKT \log T})
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} Under the decomposition assumption, the per-round gap can be telescoped through an intermediate term: writing $c^*_{i_t}$ for the best configuration of the \emph{selected} detector $i_t$, the gap between the proxy-oracle pair $(i^*_t,c^*_t)$ and the played pair $(i_t,c_t)$ splits into detector-selection suboptimality and configuration-selection suboptimality. The first term is bounded by Theorem~1 and the second by Proposition~2. Summing over $t$ and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, $\sum_i \sqrt{N_i(T)} \leq \sqrt{K \sum_i N_i(T)} = \sqrt{KT}$, gives $R_2(T) = O(\sqrt{CKT\log T})$, which combined with $R_1(T)$ yields the result. The detailed combination is given in Appendix~\ref{appendix:proofs}. \hfill $\square$

Thus, under the proxy-reward and decomposition assumptions, the average proxy regret vanishes:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{R(T)}{T} = 0
\end{equation}
Empirical support for this learning behaviour is provided in Table~\ref{tab:conv_main}, where AutoDD's proxy-oracle selection accuracy improves from 69.4\% in the first quarter of the stream to 93.1\% by stream end. This trend is consistent with the linear reward approximation on the evaluated streams, but does not by itself prove the approximation. As a concrete reference point, inverting the dominant $O(\sqrt{KmT\log T})$ term for our portfolio ($K=7$, $m=15$) to reach an average proxy suboptimality on the order of $10^{-2}$ requires a horizon of roughly $T \approx 5 \times 10^{5}$ windows, well beyond the length of any individual stream we evaluate. The convergence in Table~\ref{tab:conv_main} therefore reflects the early, steep portion of this regret curve rather than its asymptotic tail.

\section{Experiments}

We measure prequential accuracy as the percentage of correct predictions using test-then-train evaluation~\cite{Bifet2010MOA}. For synthetic datasets with known drift points, we report F1-score computed by matching detected drifts to ground truth using a tolerance window of $\tau_{\text{tol}}=100$ instances following standard practice \cite{Bifet2007ADWIN}. For real-world datasets, we report the number of detected drifts (DN). These metrics are complementary by design, as prequential accuracy captures the downstream task impact of a detector's adaptation decisions, whereas the drift-detection F1-score scores precision and recall against ground-truth drift points and thus credits timely, correct detections while penalizing both missed drifts and spurious alarms that accuracy alone would not separate. On real-world streams the drift points are unknown, so F1 cannot be computed and we instead report DN alongside accuracy; following the benchmarking guidance of Souza et al.~\cite{Souza2020Benchmark}, we interpret DN relative to the consensus detection range across methods rather than as an absolute target, treating values far above or below this range as over- and under-detection respectively. AutoDD uses all 15 meta-features with window size $w=100$, exploration parameter $\alpha_{\text{ucb}}=1.0$, regularisation parameter $\lambda_{\text{reg}}=1.0$, and $C=5$ hyperparameter configurations per detector. Results are averaged over 10 runs with random seeds uniformly sampled from the range [1, 100]. \section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
We highlight several boundaries of the current design. First, AutoDD automates detector and hyperparameter selection but not the sliding-window size $w$, which the sensitivity analysis shows to be the most influential setting; $w=100$ is robust across our benchmarks, yet selecting it for an unseen stream remains a manual step that an outer adaptive loop could close. Second, the reward is an accuracy-based proxy and therefore entangles detector quality with the downstream learner; we mitigate this through window-level smoothing and the significance threshold (Section~\ref{sec:method}), but a fully label-free reward is an open problem. Third, to isolate detection from model selection we fix the base learner to GaussianNB and HAT, which are two standard but limited families, and adapt via full model reset, while richer learners and softer adaptation strategies (warm-starting, selective parameter resets, replay, ensemble updating) are left to future work. Finally, AutoDD targets tabular data streams, the prevalent setting in the drift-detection literature; non-tabular modalities such as image, audio, and text are outside the scope of this work. Within the tabular setting, the portfolio covers the principal detector families through their strongest and most recent representatives, so that the statistical two-sample test family such as IKS~\cite{dosReis2016IKS} and WRS~\cite{Barros2018} is represented by the newer KSWIN, HDDM, and ADWIN. Because AutoDD is detector-agnostic, incorporating additional tabular detectors is a natural extension of the framework rather than a change to its selection mechanism.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We propose AutoDD, a novel framework that automatically selects and configures drift detectors for data streams. The framework extracts comprehensive meta-features and employs a two-level hierarchical selection strategy using LinUCB for detector selection and Thompson Sampling for hyperparameter configuration. Under the stated proxy-reward and linearity assumptions, the theoretical analysis gives sublinear proxy-regret bounds for the selection layer. Extensive experimental results on synthetic and real-world datasets show that AutoDD achieves consistent mean-accuracy improvements over fixed detector baselines and the compared state-of-the-art methods while requiring no expert intervention. Beyond the limitation-driven extensions discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, a further direction is multi-objective selection that jointly balances predictive accuracy, detection latency, and computational efficiency.

\section{Theoretical Proofs}\label{appendix:proofs}

This appendix gives the full proofs of the regret results stated in the Theory section. The condensed arguments in the main text (Theorem~1, Proposition~2, and the Corollary) are sketches of the derivations below.

\textbf{Preliminaries and notation.} Let $\mathcal{S} = \{(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^{\infty}$ denote the data stream, $\mathcal{D} = \{D_1, \ldots, D_K\}$ with $K=7$ the detector portfolio, and $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t \in \mathbb{R}^m$ the meta-feature vector at time $t$ with $m=15$. For LinUCB we maintain, for each detector $i$, the regularised design matrix $\mathbf{A}_{i,t} = \lambda_{\text{reg}}\mathbf{I} + \sum_{s \leq t:\, i_s=i} \boldsymbol{\phi}_s \boldsymbol{\phi}_s^\top$ and the response vector $\mathbf{b}_{i,t} = \sum_{s \leq t:\, i_s=i} r_s \boldsymbol{\phi}_s$, with ridge estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i,t} = \mathbf{A}_{i,t}^{-1} \mathbf{b}_{i,t}$. We write $N_i(T) = |\{t \leq T : i_t = i\}|$ for the number of rounds detector $i$ is selected, so that $\sum_{i=1}^K N_i(T) = T$. Throughout, A1--A3 are the assumptions of the Theory section: $\|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_2 \leq L$ with $L=\sqrt{m}$, linear reward $\mathbb{E}[r_{i,t}\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}_t] = \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*$ with $\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*\|_2 \leq S$, and $\sigma_{\text{noise}}$-sub-Gaussian noise. All bounds are stated with respect to the proxy reward of Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}, as discussed under assumption A4.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem 1 (Level 1 Proxy Regret)}

\textbf{Theorem 1.} \textit{Under assumptions A1--A3, with exploration parameter $\alpha_{\text{ucb}} = \sigma_{\text{noise}}\sqrt{m \log(KT/\delta_{\text{conf}})} + S$, LinUCB detector selection achieves with probability at least $1 - \delta_{\text{conf}}$:}
\begin{equation}
R_1(T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \max_{i} \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^* - \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_{i_t}^* \right) = O(\sqrt{KmT \log T}).
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} The proof proceeds in three steps.

\emph{Step 1: Confidence ellipsoid construction.}
By the self-normalised martingale inequality \cite{Abbasi2011Improved}, for any $\delta_{\text{conf}} > 0$ and any fixed detector $i$, with probability at least $1 - \delta_{\text{conf}}/K$, for all $t \geq 1$,
\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.95\columnwidth}{!}{$
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i,t} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i,t}} \leq \sigma_{\text{noise}}\sqrt{2\log\!\left(\frac{\det(\mathbf{A}_{i,t})^{1/2} \det(\lambda_{\text{reg}}\mathbf{I})^{-1/2}}{\delta_{\text{conf}}/K}\right)} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\text{reg}}}\,S.
$}
\end{equation}
Using the determinant--trace inequality $\det(\mathbf{A}_{i,t}) \leq \big(\tfrac{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}_{i,t})}{m}\big)^m \leq \big(\lambda_{\text{reg}} + \tfrac{tL^2}{m}\big)^m$ and a union bound over all $K$ detectors, with probability at least $1 - \delta_{\text{conf}}$, for all $i \in [K]$ and $t \geq 1$,
\begin{equation}
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i,t} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i,t}} \leq \alpha_{\text{ucb},t},
\end{equation}
where, for our choice of $\alpha_{\text{ucb}}$,
\begin{equation*}
\resizebox{0.95\columnwidth}{!}{$
\alpha_{\text{ucb},t} = \sigma_{\text{noise}}\sqrt{m\log\big(1 + \tfrac{tL^2}{\lambda_{\text{reg}} m}\big) + 2\log\tfrac{K}{\delta_{\text{conf}}}} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\text{reg}}}\,S \leq \alpha_{\text{ucb}},
$}
\end{equation*}
with the $/m$ in the log argument following from the determinant--trace bound above. Call this the \emph{confidence event}; the remainder of the proof is conditioned on it.

\emph{Step 2: Instantaneous regret bound.}
Let $i^* = \arg\max_i \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*$ be the optimal detector at time $t$, and let the UCB rule select $i_t = \arg\max_i \big( \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i,t} + \alpha_{\text{ucb}} \|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i,t}^{-1}} \big)$. Under the confidence event,
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_{i^*}^* &\leq \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i^*,t} + \alpha_{\text{ucb}} \|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i^*,t}^{-1}} \\
&\leq \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i_t,t} + \alpha_{\text{ucb}} \|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i_t,t}^{-1}} \\
&\leq \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_{i_t}^* + 2\alpha_{\text{ucb}} \|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i_t,t}^{-1}},
\end{align}
where the first and third inequalities use the confidence bound and the second uses the UCB selection rule. Hence the instantaneous regret obeys
\begin{equation}
r_t^{(1)} = \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_{i^*}^* - \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_{i_t}^* \leq 2\alpha_{\text{ucb}} \|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i_t,t}^{-1}}.
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 3: Cumulative regret via the elliptical potential lemma and the $\sqrt{K}$ factor.}
Because the portfolio uses disjoint per-detector estimators, we split the cumulative regret by the played detector and bound each subsequence separately. Fix a detector $i$ and consider the rounds $\{t : i_t = i\}$ on which it is played. By the elliptical potential lemma \cite{Dani2008Stochastic} applied to this subsequence (in its $\min(1,\cdot)$ form, so the bound holds without requiring $\lambda_{\text{reg}} \geq L^2$) and Cauchy--Schwarz,
\begin{align}
\sum_{t:\, i_t=i} \|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i,t}^{-1}}
&\leq \sqrt{N_i(T) \sum_{t:\, i_t=i} \|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i,t}^{-1}}^2} \\
&\leq \sqrt{N_i(T)\, 2m\log\!\Big(1 + \tfrac{N_i(T) L^2}{\lambda_{\text{reg}} m}\Big)}.
\end{align}
Summing the instantaneous bound over all detectors and applying Cauchy--Schwarz across detectors, $\sum_{i=1}^K \sqrt{N_i(T)} \leq \sqrt{K \sum_{i=1}^K N_i(T)} = \sqrt{KT}$, gives
\begin{align}
R_1(T) &= \sum_{i=1}^K \sum_{t:\, i_t=i} r_t^{(1)} \;\leq\; 2\alpha_{\text{ucb}} \sum_{i=1}^K \sum_{t:\, i_t=i} \|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i,t}^{-1}} \\
&\leq 2\alpha_{\text{ucb}} \sqrt{2m\log\!\Big(1 + \tfrac{T L^2}{\lambda_{\text{reg}} m}\Big)} \; \sum_{i=1}^K \sqrt{N_i(T)} \\
&\leq 2\alpha_{\text{ucb}} \sqrt{2mKT\log\!\Big(1 + \tfrac{T L^2}{\lambda_{\text{reg}} m}\Big)} \\
&= O(\sqrt{KmT \log T}),
\end{align}
where we used $\log(1 + N_i(T)L^2/(\lambda_{\text{reg}}m)) \leq \log(1 + TL^2/(\lambda_{\text{reg}}m))$ to pull the logarithmic factor out of the per-detector sum. The extra $\sqrt{K}$, relative to the single-estimator $O(\sqrt{mT\log T})$ bound, is exactly the price of maintaining $K$ separate detector estimators and aggregating their elliptical potentials.

The last line follows the standard LinUCB convention of absorbing the slowly growing confidence radius $\alpha_{\text{ucb}} = \Theta(\sqrt{m\log T})$ into the $O(\cdot)$ constant. Retaining it gives the dimension-explicit rate
\begin{equation}
R_1(T) = O\!\big(\alpha_{\text{ucb}}\sqrt{KmT\log T}\big) = \tilde{O}\big(m\sqrt{KT}\big),
\end{equation}
which is linear in $m$ and matches the canonical OFUL bound $O(d\sqrt{T}\log T)$ \cite{Abbasi2011Improved} with $d=m$; the $\sqrt{KmT\log T}$ form quoted in Theorem~1 and used for the convergence estimate in the main text is its constant-radius shorthand. This completes the proof. \hfill $\square$

\subsection{Proof of Proposition 2 (Level 2 Proxy Regret)}

\textbf{Proposition 2.} \textit{For Thompson Sampling on $C$ configurations per detector under the standard Bernoulli-sampling reduction for bounded rewards, with $N_i(T)$ selections of detector $i \in [K]$, the expected cumulative proxy regret satisfies}
\begin{equation}
R_2(T) = \sum_{i=1}^{K} O(\sqrt{C \cdot N_i(T) \log N_i(T)}).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Proof.} We analyse the hyperparameter selection problem for each detector separately.

\emph{Step 1: Reduction to a multi-armed bandit.}
Conditioned on detector $i$ being selected on the rounds $\{t : i_t = i\}$, hyperparameter selection reduces to a $C$-armed bandit over $N_i(T)$ rounds. The proxy rewards $r_t \in [-1,1]$ are normalised to $\tilde{r}_t = (r_t + 1)/2 \in [0,1]$.

\emph{Step 2: Beta--Bernoulli Thompson Sampling analysis.}
For bounded rewards in $[0,1]$, the standard reduction draws a Bernoulli outcome with mean $\tilde{r}_t$ and updates a Beta posterior with that sampled outcome. By the analysis of \cite{Agrawal2012Thompson}, Thompson Sampling with Beta$(1,1)$ priors on a $C$-armed bandit attains the gap-dependent expected regret
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[R_i(N_i)] \leq O\!\left(\sum_{c:\, \Delta_c > 0} \frac{\log N_i}{\Delta_c}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_c = \mu^* - \mu_c$ is the suboptimality gap of configuration $c$. The corresponding problem-independent (gap-free) bound, Theorem~2 of \cite{Agrawal2013Further}, is
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[R_i(N_i)] \leq O(\sqrt{C \cdot N_i(T) \log N_i(T)}).
\end{equation}
The fractional Beta update used in implementation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:ts_update}) is the conditional expectation of this Bernoulli reduction and serves as a stable implementation heuristic rather than a separate regret theorem.

\emph{Step 3: Aggregation across detectors.}
Because the detector selections partition the horizon ($\sum_{i=1}^K N_i(T) = T$) and each detector's hyperparameter problem is an independent bandit, the total Level~2 proxy regret is
\begin{equation}
R_2(T) = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \mathbb{E}[R_i] = \sum_{i=1}^{K} O(\sqrt{C \cdot N_i(T) \log N_i(T)}).
\end{equation}
This completes the proof. \hfill $\square$

\subsection{Proof of the Corollary (Combined Proxy Regret)}

\textbf{Corollary.} \textit{Under the decomposition $R(T) = R_1(T) + R_2(T)$, where $R_1$ captures detector-selection suboptimality and $R_2$ configuration-selection suboptimality, the combined proxy regret is}
\begin{equation}
R(T) = O(\sqrt{KmT \log T} + \sqrt{CKT \log T}).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Proof.} We combine the two bounds. From Theorem~1, $R_1(T) = O(\sqrt{KmT \log T})$. For $R_2(T)$, applying $\log N_i(T) \leq \log T$ and Cauchy--Schwarz across detectors,
\begin{align}
R_2(T) &= \sum_{i=1}^{K} O(\sqrt{C \cdot N_i(T) \log N_i(T)}) \\
&\leq O\!\left(\sqrt{C \log T}\, \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sqrt{N_i(T)}\right) \\
&\leq O\!\left(\sqrt{C \log T}\, \sqrt{K \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{K} N_i(T)}\right) \\
&= O(\sqrt{CKT \log T}),
\end{align}
using $\sum_i \sqrt{N_i(T)} \leq \sqrt{K \sum_i N_i(T)}$ and $\sum_i N_i(T) = T$. Adding the two terms yields $R(T) = O(\sqrt{KmT \log T} + \sqrt{CKT \log T})$. As in Theorem~1, the $R_1$ term is quoted in its constant-radius shorthand; carrying $\alpha_{\text{ucb}}$ replaces $\sqrt{KmT\log T}$ by $\tilde{O}(m\sqrt{KT})$, whereas the $R_2$ term needs no such convention since the Thompson-sampling constant is universal. Since both terms are sublinear in $T$, the average proxy regret satisfies $\lim_{T\to\infty} R(T)/T = 0$. This completes the proof. \hfill $\square$
