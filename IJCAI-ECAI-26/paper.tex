\begin{abstract}
Concept drift, the change in the distribution of streaming data over time, significantly impacts the performance of machine learning models. While numerous drift detection methods have been proposed, selecting and configuring the most suitable detector for a specific data stream remains a challenging task. Existing AutoML frameworks for streaming data that automate model selection rely on fixed drift detection strategies, leaving a critical gap in automation. This paper introduces \textbf{AutoDD} (\textbf{A}utomated \textbf{D}rift \textbf{D}etector), a novel framework that jointly automates detector selection and hyperparameter configuration using a hierarchical learning approach. AutoDD extracts comprehensive online meta-features and employs a two-level hierarchical selection algorithm to adaptively choose both the detector and its configuration. Theoretical analysis provides sublinear regret bounds ensuring convergence to optimal performance. Comprehensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that AutoDD consistently outperforms state-of-the-art detectors while maintaining computational efficiency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Concept drift, where the statistical properties of streaming data evolve over time, poses a significant challenge in data stream mining \cite{Gama2014Survey}, incremental learning \cite{Losing2018IncrementalOnline}, and non-stationary learning \cite{Lu2019Learning}. As data distributions change, machine learning models experience degraded performance, necessitating timely detection and adaptation. This phenomenon manifests in various forms, including sudden (abrupt) drift, gradual drift, incremental drift, and recurring drift, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:type_of_drift}. For instance, an electricity price prediction model may encounter sudden drift when market regulations change abruptly, experience gradual drift as consumer behavior shifts over seasons, or observe recurring drift driven by daily demand cycles~\cite{Lu2019Learning}.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\columnwidth]{images/type_of_drift_detection.png}
    \caption{Types of concept drifts.}
    \label{fig:type_of_drift}
\end{figure}

Over the past two decades, numerous drift detection methods have been developed to address this challenge, including error rate-based approaches such as DDM \cite{Gama2004DDM}, ADWIN \cite{Bifet2007ADWIN}, and ADDM \cite{Mayaki2022ADDM}, distribution-based methods like KSWIN \cite{Raab2020KSWIN}, uncertainty-based methods such as PUDD \cite{Lu2025PUDD}, and meta-learning techniques like Meta-ADD \cite{Yu2021MetaADD}. However, a fundamental challenge remains unresolved in systematically selecting and configuring the most appropriate drift detector for a given data stream.

Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) has revolutionized the field by addressing similar selection challenges in batch learning scenarios \cite{Hutter2019AutoML}. Recent advancements have extended AutoML to streaming data, enabling automatic selection and configuration of classifiers \cite{Celik2023OAML,Verma2024ASML}. However, these systems treat drift detection as a fixed component, requiring manual specification and leaving a critical gap in the automation framework.

To bridge this gap, we introduce \textbf{AutoDD} (\textbf{A}utomated \textbf{D}rift \textbf{D}etector). This modular framework automates drift detector selection and configuration using a hierarchical learning approach, compatible with any model or AutoML pipeline.

\begin{itemize}
\item We propose a two-level hierarchical selection algorithm using LinUCB \cite{Li2010LinUCB} and Thompson Sampling \cite{Agrawal2012Thompson} that adaptively selects detectors and their hyperparameters based on online meta-features.
\item We formalize detector selection with a theoretical analysis that provides sublinear regret bounds guaranteeing convergence to optimal selections.
\item Extensive evaluation demonstrates AutoDD outperforms state-of-the-art detectors while maintaining computational efficiency.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the experimental results and analysis. %Section~\ref{sec:discussion} addresses key design decisions and limitations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Drift detection methods can be broadly categorized into three paradigms. \textbf{Error rate-based methods} monitor fluctuations in classifier error rates, triggering alarms when changes are statistically significant. DDM \cite{Gama2004DDM} pioneered the warning-drift framework by monitoring prediction accuracy under binomial distribution assumptions, followed by EDDM \cite{BaenaGarcia2006EDDM} which focuses on distances between errors to detect gradual drift, HDDM \cite{FriasBlanco2015Hoeffding} which applies Hoeffding's inequality with exponentially weighted moving averages, and ADWIN \cite{Bifet2007ADWIN} which maintains adaptive sliding windows that automatically shrink when distribution changes are detected. Page-Hinkley \cite{Page1954Continuous} applies cumulative sum control charts to detect mean shifts in sequential data. Recent advances include MDDM \cite{Pesaranghader2017MDDM}, which uses McDiarmid's inequality for shorter detection delays with tighter concentration bounds, and ADDM \cite{Mayaki2022ADDM}, which models error rates as SETAR time series \cite{Tsay1989TestingAM} to achieve lower false alarm rates. \textbf{Distribution-based methods} compare data distributions across windows by quantifying dissimilarity between new and historical data distributions \cite{Kifer2004ChangeDetection}, including approaches based on information theory \cite{Dasu2006InformationTheoretic}, competence models \cite{Lu2014CompetenceModels}, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (KSWIN) \cite{Raab2020KSWIN} which detects changes in univariate feature distributions. \textbf{Uncertainty and learning-based methods} address limitations of error-rate monitoring: PUDD \cite{Lu2025PUDD} leverages prediction confidence for earlier detection by monitoring changes in prediction uncertainty distributions, while Meta-ADD \cite{Yu2021MetaADD} employs prototypical networks to classify drift types via meta-learning on diverse drift scenarios.

Recent developments in automated algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimization (CASH) \cite{Hutter2019AutoML} have been extended to streaming scenarios~\cite{kulbach2022evolution}. 
However, all existing streaming AutoML systems assume a pre-selected drift detector, requiring manual specification and leaving a critical gap in the automation framework. Despite advances in both drift detection and streaming AutoML, drift detector selection remains manual. Our work fills this gap by extending AutoML to automatic drift detector selection and configuration.


\section{Automated Drift Detector}
\label{sec:method}

AutoDD employs an adaptive, closed-loop architecture to dynamically select and configure drift detectors. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}, this architecture operates through several key steps. First, the system initializes the detector portfolio with standard hyperparameters and sets up the learning parameters. Then, during online operation, meta-features characterizing stream velocity, stationarity, and temporal patterns are continuously extracted from the incoming data stream. These meta-features serve as context for the LinUCB agent, which selects the most appropriate detector from the portfolio. Subsequently, Thompson Sampling configures the selected detector's hyperparameters for optimal performance. The configured detector monitors the stream and triggers model adaptation upon detecting drift. Finally, performance feedback is computed based on detection outcomes, which updates both the LinUCB and Thompson Sampling components to refine future selections. This adaptive learning cycle enables AutoDD to continuously improve its detector selection over time.

\subsection{Algorithm Overview}

AutoDD operates over sliding windows of size $w$. When each window fills with incoming instances, the framework executes a complete selection cycle, beginning with meta-feature extraction.

\textbf{Meta-Feature Extraction:} The meta-feature set comprises 15 features organized into three categories - Statistical, Stationarity, and Temporal. Table~\ref{tab:metafeatures} summarizes each meta-feature with its mathematical formulation. The resulting context vector $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ with $m=15$ is computed incrementally, ensuring optimum update complexity per instance.

\subsection{Hierarchical Learning Approach}

AutoDD uses a two-level hierarchical selection strategy that jointly optimizes detector choice and hyperparameter configuration while maintaining computational efficiency. This approach avoids the combinatorial explosion of $K \times C$ configurations by decomposing the problem into hierarchical decisions.

\textbf{Level 1 - Detector Selection via LinUCB:} We formulate detector selection as a contextual multi-armed bandit problem where context is $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t$ (meta-features), arms are $K$ detectors, and reward is detection performance. LinUCB \cite{Li2010LinUCB} assumes linear reward:
\begin{equation}
r_{i,t} = \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i + \epsilon_t
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the unknown parameter vector for detector $i$, with $m$ being the meta-feature dimensionality. The algorithm selects the detector with the highest upper confidence bound:
\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.9\columnwidth}{!}{$
i_t = \arg\max_{i \in [K]} \left( \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i + \alpha_{\text{ucb}} \sqrt{\boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \mathbf{A}_i^{-1} \boldsymbol{\phi}_t} \right)
$}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i = \mathbf{A}_i^{-1} \mathbf{b}_i$ is the ridge regression estimate. Here, $\mathbf{A}_i = \lambda_{\text{reg}}\mathbf{I} + \sum_{s:i_s=i} \boldsymbol{\phi}_s \boldsymbol{\phi}_s^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is the regularized design matrix with regularization parameter $\lambda_{\text{reg}} > 0$, and $\mathbf{b}_i = \sum_{s:i_s=i} r_s \boldsymbol{\phi}_s \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the cumulative reward-weighted context vector for detector $i$. The parameter $\alpha_{\text{ucb}} > 0$ controls exploration-exploitation balance.

\textbf{Level 2 - Hyperparameter Selection via Thompson Sampling:} Given the selected detector $D_{i_t}$, we adaptively configure its hyperparameters using Thompson Sampling \cite{Agrawal2012Thompson}. For each configuration $c \in [C] = \{1, \ldots, C\}$ of detector $i$, we maintain Beta distribution parameters $(a_{i,c}, b_{i,c})$ initialized to $(1, 1)$. At each window, we sample:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ts_sample}
\psi_{i_t,c} \sim \text{Beta}(a_{i_t,c}, b_{i_t,c}) \quad \forall c \in [C]
\end{equation}
and select configuration:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ts_select}
c_t = \arg\max_c \psi_{i_t,c}
\end{equation}

After observing reward $r_t$, we update:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:ts_update}
a_{i_t,c_t} &\gets a_{i_t,c_t} + \tilde{r}_t \\
b_{i_t,c_t} &\gets b_{i_t,c_t} + (1 - \tilde{r}_t)
\end{align}
where $\tilde{r}_t = (r_t + 1)/2$ normalizes the reward to $[0,1]$. %This cascading approach provides tractability (only $K$ arms at Level 1), specialization (each detector's hyperparameters tuned independently), and fast adaptation (Thompson Sampling quickly identifies effective configurations).

\subsection{Evaluation and Adaptation}

Evaluating detector performance without ground truth drift labels is challenging. Following standard practice, we use model accuracy drop as a proxy metric. The reward at time $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t = \begin{cases}
0.5 + \min(\Delta a_t \cdot 2, 0.5) & d_t=1, \Delta a_t > \tau_{\text{sig}} \\
-0.3 & d_t=1, \Delta a_t \leq \tau_{\text{sig}} \\
-0.5 - \min(\Delta a_t, 0.5) & d_t=0, \Delta a_t > \tau_{\text{sig}} \\
0.1 & d_t=0, \Delta a_t \leq \tau_{\text{sig}}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $d_t \in \{0,1\}$ indicates drift detection at time $t$, $\Delta a_t = a_{t-w} - a_t$ is the accuracy drop between consecutive windows, and $\tau_{\text{sig}} = 0.05$ is the significance threshold. The reward function penalizes both false positives and false negatives while rewarding correct drift detections and stable periods.

Upon drift detection, the model undergoes a complete reset. Let $\mathcal{M}_t$ denote the model state at time $t$. When drift is detected ($d_t = 1$), we perform:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}_t \gets \mathcal{M}_0
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}_0$ represents the initial untrained state. The model then resumes incremental learning from subsequent instances:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}_{t+s} \gets \text{Update}(\mathcal{M}_{t+s-1}, (\mathbf{x}_{t+s}, y_{t+s})) \quad \forall s > 0
\end{equation}

The complete procedure is formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:autodd}.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
\caption{AutoDD Algorithm}
\label{alg:autodd}
\textbf{Input:} Stream $\mathcal{S}$, Portfolio $\mathcal{D}$, Window $w$\\
\textbf{Parameter:} Exploration $\alpha_{\text{ucb}}$, Regularization $\lambda_{\text{reg}}$, Configs per detector $C$\\
\textbf{Output:} Drift signals $\{d_t\}_{t=1}^\infty$
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Initialize $\mathbf{A}_i \gets \lambda_{\text{reg}}\mathbf{I}_m$, $\mathbf{b}_i \gets \mathbf{0}$ for all $i \in [K]$
\STATE Initialize $a_{i,c} \gets 1$, $b_{i,c} \gets 1$ for all $i \in [K], c \in [C]$
\STATE Initialize model $\mathcal{M}_0$, sliding window $W \gets \emptyset$
\FOR{each instance $(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t) \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \STATE $W \gets W \cup \{(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t)\}$
    \IF{$|W| = w$}
        \STATE $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t \gets \textsc{Extract}(W)$ \COMMENT{Extract meta-features}
        %\STATE \textit{// Level 1: Detector Selection (LinUCB)}
        \FOR{$i = 1$ to $K$}
            \STATE $\text{UCB}_i \gets \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \mathbf{A}_i^{-1} \mathbf{b}_i + \alpha_{\text{ucb}} \sqrt{\boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \mathbf{A}_i^{-1} \boldsymbol{\phi}_t}$
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE $i_t \gets \arg\max_i \text{UCB}_i$ \COMMENT{Select detector}
        %\STATE \textit{// Level 2: Hyperparameter Selection (Thompson Sampling)}
        \FOR{$c = 1$ to $C$}
            \STATE Sample $\psi_c \sim \text{Beta}(a_{i_t,c}, b_{i_t,c})$
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE $c_t \gets \arg\max_c \psi_c$ \COMMENT{Select configuration}
        \STATE $d_t \gets D_{i_t}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i_t,c_t}).\textsc{Detect}(W)$
        \IF{$d_t = 1$}
            \STATE $\mathcal{M}_t \gets \mathcal{M}_0$ %\textit{// Reset model to initial state}
        \ENDIF
        \STATE \COMMENT{Compute reward $r_t$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}}
        %\STATE \textit{// Update Level 1 (LinUCB)}
        \STATE $\mathbf{A}_{i_t} \gets \mathbf{A}_{i_t} + \boldsymbol{\phi}_t \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top$; $\mathbf{b}_{i_t} \gets \mathbf{b}_{i_t} + r_t \boldsymbol{\phi}_t$
        %\STATE \textit{// Update Level 2 (Thompson Sampling)}
        \STATE $\tilde{r}_t \gets (r_t + 1)/2$
        \STATE $a_{i_t,c_t} \gets a_{i_t,c_t} + \tilde{r}_t$; $b_{i_t,c_t} \gets b_{i_t,c_t} + (1-\tilde{r}_t)$
        \STATE $W \gets \emptyset$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Computational Complexity}
AutoDD has per-window complexity of $O(wd + Km^2)$, where $w$ is the AutoDD window size, $d$ is the input feature dimensionality, $K$ is the number of detectors, and $m=15$ is the meta-feature count. The dominant costs are meta-feature extraction ($O(wd)$) and LinUCB matrix operations ($O(Km^2)$). Space complexity is $O(Km^2 + wd)$ for storing $K$ covariance matrices of size $m \times m$ and the sliding window buffer.

\subsection{Theoretical Analysis}

We formalize the optimization objective as a streaming detector selection problem and provide regret guarantees for the hierarchical learning approach. Our analysis focuses on the selection mechanism, demonstrating that AutoDD effectively converges to the optimal drift detector and hyperparameter configuration from the available portfolio.\footnote{Complete proofs are provided in the supplementary material.}

\textbf{Problem Formulation.} A data stream $\mathcal{S} = \{(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^{\infty}$ exhibits concept drift when distributions change over time:
\begin{equation}
\exists t, t' : P_t(\mathbf{x}, y) \neq P_{t'}(\mathbf{x}, y)
\end{equation}

Given a detector portfolio $\mathcal{D} = \{D_1, \ldots, D_K\}$ where each $D_i$ has configuration space $\mathcal{H}_i$, AutoDD addresses the streaming detector selection and configuration problem. Inspired by the CASH formulation \cite{Hutter2019AutoML}, we jointly optimize the detector choice and its hyperparameters:
\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.95\columnwidth}{!}{$
(D^*_t, \boldsymbol{\eta}^*_t) = \arg\max_{D_i \in \mathcal{D}, \boldsymbol{\eta} \in \mathcal{H}_i} \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}(D_i(\boldsymbol{\eta}), \mathcal{S}_t) \mid \boldsymbol{\phi}_t]
$}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t$ is the stream context, $\mathcal{S}_t$ denotes the stream segment at time $t$, $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ is the expectation operator, and $\mathcal{M}$ denotes the downstream model performance.

\textbf{Assumptions.} We adopt three standard assumptions from the contextual bandit literature \cite{Abbasi2011Improved,Lattimore2020Bandit}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{(A1) Bounded features:} $\|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_2 \leq L$ for all $t$, satisfied by $[0,1]$ normalization with $L=\sqrt{m}$.
\item \textbf{(A2) Linear reward:} For each detector $i$, there exists $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^* \in \mathbb{R}^m$ with $\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*\|_2 \leq S$ such that $\mathbb{E}[r_{i,t} \mid \boldsymbol{\phi}_t] = \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*$, where $S > 0$ is a constant upper bound on the parameter norms.
\item \textbf{(A3) Sub-Gaussian noise:} Reward noise is $\sigma_{\text{noise}}$-sub-Gaussian, satisfied with $\sigma_{\text{noise}} = 1$ for bounded rewards in $[-1,1]$.
\end{itemize}

The following result establishes that LinUCB learns optimal detector selection with sublinearly diminishing regret.

\textbf{Theorem 1 (Level 1 Regret).} Under assumptions A1--A3, with exploration parameter $\alpha_{\text{ucb}} = \sigma_{\text{noise}}\sqrt{m \log(KT/\delta_{\text{conf}})} + S$ where $T$ is the time horizon and $\delta_{\text{conf}} \in (0,1)$ is the confidence parameter, LinUCB detector selection achieves with probability at least $1 - \delta_{\text{conf}}$:
\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.95\columnwidth}{!}{$
R_1(T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \max_{i} \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^* - \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_{i_t}^* \right) = O(\sqrt{mT \log T})
$}
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} The proof follows directly from Theorem 2 of \cite{Abbasi2011Improved}. By the self-normalized martingale inequality (Theorem 1, \cite{Abbasi2011Improved}), the true parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i^*$ lies within the confidence ellipsoid $\mathcal{C}_{i,t} = \{\boldsymbol{\theta}: \|\boldsymbol{\theta} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i\|_{\mathbf{A}_i} \leq \alpha_{\text{ucb}}\}$ with probability $1-\delta_{\text{conf}}$. Under this event, UCB selection ensures $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_{i_t}^* \geq \max_i \boldsymbol{\phi}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\theta}_i^* - 2\alpha_{\text{ucb}}\|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i_t}^{-1}}$. Summing over all rounds and applying the elliptical potential lemma \cite{Dani2008Stochastic} yields $\sum_{t=1}^T \|\boldsymbol{\phi}_t\|_{\mathbf{A}_{i_t}^{-1}} \leq \sqrt{2mT\log(1+TL^2/m)}$, establishing the stated bound. \hfill $\square$

Thompson Sampling subsequently balances exploration and exploitation for hyperparameter configuration.

\textbf{Theorem 2 (Level 2 Regret).} For Thompson Sampling on $C$ configurations per detector, with $N_i(T)$ denoting the number of times detector $i \in [K]$ is selected over $T$ rounds, the cumulative regret satisfies:
\begin{equation}
R_2(T) = \sum_{i=1}^{K} O(\sqrt{C \cdot N_i(T) \log N_i(T)})
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} For each detector $i \in [K]$, conditioned on detector $i$ being selected, hyperparameter selection reduces to a $C$-armed bandit with bounded rewards. We normalize $r_t \in [-1,1]$ to $\tilde{r}_t = (r_t+1)/2 \in [0,1]$ and maintain Beta posteriors updated with $\tilde{r}_t$. By Theorem 1 of \cite{Agrawal2013Further}, Thompson Sampling achieves expected regret $O(\sqrt{CN_i(T)\log N_i(T)})$ over $N_i(T)$ rounds. Since $\sum_i N_i(T) = T$, summing across all detectors yields the stated bound. \hfill $\square$

The combined regret remains sublinear, ensuring that AutoDD's average per-round suboptimality vanishes over time.

\textbf{Corollary (Combined Regret).} Let $(i^*, c^*)$ denote the optimal detector-configuration pair. The total regret decomposes as $R(T) = R_1(T) + R_2(T)$, where $R_1(T)$ captures suboptimality from detector selection and $R_2(T)$ from configuration selection. The combined bound is:
\begin{equation}
R(T) = O(\sqrt{mT \log T} + \sqrt{CKT \log T})
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, $\sum_i \sqrt{N_i(T)} \leq \sqrt{K \sum_i N_i(T)} = \sqrt{KT}$. Thus $R_2(T) = O(\sqrt{CKT\log T})$, and combining with $R_1(T)$ yields the result. \hfill $\square$

This sublinear regret bound guarantees convergence to optimal detector-configuration pairs:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{R(T)}{T} = 0
\end{equation}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We propose AutoDD, a novel framework that automatically selects and configures drift detectors for data streams. The framework extracts comprehensive meta-features and employs a two-level hierarchical selection strategy using LinUCB for detector selection and Thompson Sampling for hyperparameter configuration. Theoretical analysis provides sublinear regret bounds guaranteeing convergence to optimal detector-configuration pairs. Extensive experimental results on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that AutoDD achieves considerable improvements over fixed detector baselines and state-of-the-art methods while requiring no expert intervention. Future work includes incorporating multi-objective optimization to balance accuracy, detection latency, and computational efficiency. We also plan to expand AutoDD to different types of data stream problems, including unsupervised drift detection scenarios where labels are unavailable. Additionally, exploring better adaptation strategies beyond model reset, such as incremental learning and ensemble updating, represents a promising direction for further improving post-drift recovery.
